Soil & Pest Care

Healthy soil and timely pest management support stronger crops, reduce avoidable costs, and protect the environment. Start by observing the field and confirming the likely cause before choosing an action.

Helpful steps

  • Test soil and, when salinity is suspected, irrigation water; use results and local recommendations to guide inputs.
  • Protect soil with suitable rotations, crop cover, organic matter, and careful water management.
  • Inspect several plants, including both sides of leaves, and confirm the cause before treatment.
  • Use Integrated Pest Management (IPM): prevention and monitoring first, with pesticides only when needed and label-approved.

Yellow leaves, wilting, or poor growth are not proof of pests: water stress, nutrient problems, weather, and disease can look similar. For an unfamiliar or spreading issue, share clear photos and crop details with a local agricultural extension officer, Krishi Vigyan Kendra (KVK), or qualified agronomist.

What makes soil healthy—and what can go wrong?

Soil supports roots and contains mineral particles, organic matter, water, air, and living organisms. Healthy soil has useful structure and drainage, enough organic matter, balanced nutrients, and a pH and salinity level suitable for the crop.

  • Erosion: rain or wind can carry away exposed topsoil. Keep the ground covered and manage runoff; use contour or terrace methods where locally suitable.
  • Fertility loss: repeated cropping can deplete nutrients. Test soil, follow recommendations, add suitable well-decomposed organic matter, and rotate crops.
  • Compaction: machinery or working wet soil can restrict roots and drainage. Avoid unnecessary traffic, especially when soil is wet.
  • Waterlogging or poor drainage: standing water can damage roots. Improve drainage where feasible and avoid over-irrigation.
  • Salinity or unsuitable pH: poor germination, stunting, leaf-edge burn, or nutrient uptake problems may occur. Confirm with soil or water testing before following local advice.
  • Low organic matter: soil may crust or hold less water. Where practical, use suitable residues, cover crops, or compost instead of burning residues.

A soil test may report pH, electrical conductivity (salinity), organic matter, and available nutrients. Take a representative sample using your local laboratory’s instructions; test unusual spots separately when needed. Do not apply fertiliser, lime, or gypsum solely by guesswork.

Ways to maintain soil health
  • Test soil through a recognised service; test irrigation water if salinity is suspected.
  • Base fertiliser and amendment decisions on test results, crop needs, and local recommendations—more fertiliser is not always better.
  • Rotate crop families where possible, and include legumes when suitable for the region and farm.
  • Use suitable residues, mulch, cover crops, or vegetation to reduce erosion and evaporation.
  • Irrigate according to crop needs and soil moisture to reduce both drought stress and waterlogging.
  • Protect soil life by reducing unnecessary disturbance and avoiding contamination.
Recognise crop symptoms and use IPM

Crop damage can come from insects, mites, rodents, slugs, nematodes, fungi, bacteria, viruses, weeds, nutrient problems, or water stress. Signs may include holes or chewed leaf edges, curling or sticky leaves, webbing, spots, powdery growth, rotting, wilting, damaged roots or stems, and fruit holes.

Integrated Pest Management (IPM) combines monitoring and prevention with cultural, mechanical, biological, and—only when necessary—chemical controls. The aim is to keep damage at an acceptable level while protecting people, beneficial organisms, and the environment.

  • Monitor and identify: inspect several plants, leaf tops and undersides, stems, and fruits; use traps where suitable.
  • Prevent: use healthy seed or seedlings, clean tools where appropriate, manage weeds, and remove badly affected residues safely.
  • Use cultural methods: rotate crops, maintain suitable spacing, avoid excess nitrogen, and manage irrigation.
  • Use mechanical methods where appropriate: hand-pick larger pests, remove affected parts, or use barriers and traps.
  • Protect natural enemies and use biological-control products only when registered, suitable, and locally recommended.
  • Use pesticides only when needed, and only if registered for the crop and target pest.

Repeatedly growing the same crop, warm or humid weather, infested seed or nearby host weeds, late detection, and incorrect identification can all make problems worse. Some pests and diseases are weather- and crop-specific.

What to do when a crop looks damaged
  1. Photograph the whole plant, affected area, and any insect or visible sign; include the crop name and growth stage.
  2. Inspect more than one plant and both sides of leaves. Note where symptoms occur and when they began.
  3. Review recent irrigation, rainfall, weather, fertiliser, and pesticide use.
  4. Do not spray an unidentified chemical or increase a dose. Seek local expert help if the cause is unclear or damage is spreading.
  5. After an action, inspect again and record whether the damage is increasing or decreasing.

Example: yellow leaves

Before assuming insects are responsible, check whether pests are visible, whether soil is too wet or dry, whether older or newer leaves are affected, and whether fertiliser or pesticide was recently applied. Possible causes include nutrient deficiency, water stress, pests, or disease. Treat these as possibilities and seek expert confirmation when unclear.

Use crop-protection products safely
  • Follow the product label and local regulations. Use only products registered for the crop and target pest.
  • Wear the protective equipment specified on the label; keep children and animals away during mixing and application.
  • Avoid spraying against the wind, near water sources, or in conditions prohibited by the label; prevent drift onto neighbouring crops.
  • Observe the pre-harvest interval before picking and the re-entry interval before returning to treated fields.
  • Never reuse pesticide containers for food or water. Dispose of containers and leftover product according to the label and local guidance.
  • Do not repeatedly rely on one pesticide. Ask a qualified adviser about resistance management; never mix products unless labels permit it.
Share useful details when asking for digital or expert help

Include the crop, growth stage, district or region, soil-test results if available, clear photos, and recent weather and farm practices. Describe where symptoms occur and what you have already tried. Treat photo-based or app-generated causes as possibilities—not a guaranteed diagnosis—and confirm costly or uncertain actions with a local expert.

This is general educational guidance, not a diagnosis or a substitute for crop-specific local advice. Exact chemical recommendations, doses, and treatment schedules must come from current, locally approved agricultural guidance.
